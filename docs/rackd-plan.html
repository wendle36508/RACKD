Swipe to thrift: a plan for the app

You want an endless deck of second-hand listings from Depop, Vinted and eBay. Swipe right on what you love, left on what you don't, and the app learns your style until every card feels picked for you. Here's how to build it legally, cheaply and without buying data.

1 · Where the listings come from

Getting listings without getting sued

Scraping means a program visits a website like a robot and copies what's on the page. It's technically easy, and lots of people sell ready-made scrapers for Depop and Vinted. But for a startup that wants investors and a long life, it's the riskiest way to start.

An API is the official "side door" a company opens so other apps can request its data in a tidy format, with permission. That's what you want.

Why not just scrape Depop and Vinted?

  • Breaking the rules you agreed to. Their terms of service ban automated collection. In the US, the hiQ v. LinkedIn case showed that even when scraping public pages isn't a crime, the company can still win on breach of contract.
  • Personal data. Listings include seller usernames and photos of real people. In the UK and EU, that falls under data-protection law (GDPR), which needs a legal reason to collect it.
  • Copyright. Sellers own their photos. Copying them onto your servers is different from linking to them.
  • It breaks. Sites change their pages and block scrapers constantly, so your feed would go dark without warning.
  • You'd burn the bridge. Depop and Vinted are your best future partners. Showing up as "the startup that scraped us" kills that conversation.
The pitch you'll eventually make to Depop and Vinted

"We send you buyers who already love the item, because they swiped right on it. We only show your listings and link straight back to you." That's free, qualified traffic for them. Once you have a few hundred active users on eBay listings, you have the numbers to make that pitch.

2 · How the app learns your style

Teaching the app your taste

Here's the key idea. There are free, ready-made AI models (the best-known is called CLIP) that can look at a photo and turn it into a list of a few hundred numbers. That list is called an embedding. Think of it as a coordinate on a giant map of style, where photos that look alike land close together. A baggy 90s windbreaker lands near other 90s sportswear. A slip dress lands near other minimal, silky pieces.

So the app never needs to be told "this is grunge". It already sees which items look alike. Your taste then becomes a spot on that same map:

  • Swipe right: your spot moves a little toward that item.
  • Swipe left: your spot moves a little away from it.
  • Next card: the app mostly picks items close to your spot, plus about 1 in 5 that are a bit further away. This "exploring" stops your feed getting boring and lets it find tastes you didn't know you had.

Solving the "first day" problem

A brand-new user has swiped nothing, so the app knows nothing. This is called the cold start. Fix it with a 60-second onboarding quiz:

  • Show 10 to 15 photos covering very different styles (Y2K, gorpcore, vintage workwear, minimal, streetwear, cottagecore and so on). The user taps the ones they like. That sets their starting spot on the map.
  • Ask the boring but vital stuff: sizes, budget, gender of clothing, which platforms they'll buy from, and country. Nothing kills a swipe app faster than loving an item that doesn't fit.
  • Optional later: "Paste a Pinterest board" or "upload 3 outfit photos" to set the starting spot instantly.
Later, once you have thousands of users

You can add "people who liked what you liked also liked this", the trick Spotify and Netflix use. That's when your own swipe data becomes your biggest advantage, and nobody else has it.

3 · Should you buy data?

Short answer: no

  • What you'd need doesn't exist for sale. The useful data is "this person liked these second-hand listings and disliked those". Nobody sells that. It's exactly what your app will create.
  • The photo-reading AI already does the heavy lifting. CLIP-style models learned from hundreds of millions of images, for free. That's the "bought data", already paid for.
  • Public fashion datasets (like DeepFashion) are mostly licensed for research only, not commercial apps. Read the licence before touching one.
  • Buying personal data about people is legally risky in the UK and EU unless they clearly agreed to it being sold to you. It's also a bad look for investors.
  • Better use of money: get your first 100 users. Post in thrift communities on TikTok, Reddit and Discord, or offer a small prize to beta testers who swipe 200 cards. Real users give you real data.

5 · Roadmap

From idea to Ignite demo

  1. Week 1

    Check the problem is real

    Talk to 15 to 20 thrifters. Ask how long they scroll, how often they give up, what they'd pay. Sign up for an eBay developer account and the eBay Partner Network (approval can take a few days).

  2. Weeks 2–4

    Build the prototype

    eBay listings only, one category (say tops and jackets), one country. Style quiz, swipe deck, a "liked" list with Buy buttons. That's the whole app.

  3. Weeks 5–8

    Private beta with 50–100 users

    Track three numbers: what share of swipes are right swipes, and does it rise as people keep swiping (proof the app learns); how many tap Buy; how many come back next week.

  4. Month 3

    Ignite pitch

    Show the "right-swipe rate climbs from swipe 1 to swipe 100" chart. That one graph proves the core idea better than any slide.

  5. Month 3+

    Approach Depop and Vinted

    Bring your user numbers and click-through stats. Ask for a data feed or partner API in exchange for the buyers you send.

A realistic money note

Commission on second-hand clothes is small per sale (a 3% cut of a $30 jacket is about 90 cents). It proves the model and covers early costs. The bigger money is usually the subscription and seller boosts once you have an engaged audience.

Honest mentor note: swipes are not the same as listings scraped

Every scrape is shared by all users, so 1,000 users don't cost 1,000 times more. But a paid user who really swipes 400 new cards in an hour needs at least 400 fresh listings arriving every hour. The Launch plan only brings in about 80, and even the Bigger plan brings in about 200. Delivering the full 400 needs the "Full paid tier" row, about $735 a month, which roughly 160 subscribers would cover.

Two things soften this. "New" can mean "new to you", so older listings a user hasn't seen yet still count. And 400 an hour is one swipe every 9 seconds for a full hour, so most people won't hit it. Start on Launch, and only scale the scraper when paying users actually run out of cards.

7 · Watch out for

Things that can trip you up

  • Read the eBay API rules. They cover how you show listings, how long you can keep data, and that you link back to eBay. Following them keeps your access.
  • Sold items. Second-hand listings are one-offs. Refresh often so people don't fall for something that's already gone.
  • Privacy policy from day one. You're storing what people like. Say what you collect and why, and let them delete it.
  • Depending on one platform. eBay could change its rules. That's another reason to start partner conversations early.

This is a starting plan, not legal advice. Before launch, have someone check the eBay API licence and your privacy policy against the rules in your country.
